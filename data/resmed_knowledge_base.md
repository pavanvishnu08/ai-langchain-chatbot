## What is sleep apnea?
Sleep apnea is a disorder where breathing repeatedly stops and starts (apneas) or becomes shallow (hypopneas) during sleep, cutting off oxygen and fragmenting sleep. According to ResMed's site, someone with sleep apnea can wake up 240+ times across a full eight hours of sleep. This leaves people exhausted, low-energy, and often reliant on caffeine during the day.

## Types of sleep apnea
- Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA): the most common type, affecting nearly one billion people globally. It happens when throat muscles relax and narrow the airway, restricting airflow; the brain then signals a wake-up to resume breathing, sometimes many times a night. Contributing factors include alcohol use, smoking, narcotic drug use, excess weight, age, enlarged tonsils/adenoids, a narrow airway, a receding chin, or a deviated septum.
- Central Sleep Apnea (CSA): less common; happens when the brain fails to properly signal the breathing muscles, causing pauses in breathing. Can be linked to high altitude, narcotic drug use, or conditions like heart failure or stroke.
- Complex/Treatment-Emergent Central Sleep Apnea (TECSA): a form of central sleep apnea that can emerge early in OSA treatment; it often resolves on its own without extra intervention.

## Sleep apnea symptoms
Common symptoms listed on ResMed's site include: snoring, waking up gasping for air, difficulty concentrating during the day, persistent daytime tiredness, morning headaches, difficulty staying asleep, waking with a dry mouth, and irritability/mood swings. ResMed notes that roughly 35% of people with sleep apnea also show symptoms of depression.

## Sleep apnea risk factors
Key risk factors highlighted by ResMed: obesity/excess weight (which narrows the airway), being male (moderate-to-severe sleep-disordered breathing affects about 49.7% of men vs. 23.4% of women, though risk rises for women during pregnancy and after menopause), age over 40, larger neck circumference (17+ inches in men, 16+ inches in women), and smoking or alcohol use.

## Long-term effects if untreated
If left untreated, sleep apnea can contribute to heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, and cognitive decline, on top of disrupting mood and daily energy. Early diagnosis and treatment are recommended to reduce these risks.

## How sleep apnea is diagnosed
A doctor typically evaluates symptoms first, then may refer the patient to a sleep specialist. Diagnosis usually involves one of two tests:
- Home Sleep Test (HST): done conveniently from the patient's own bed, usually for people with fairly straightforward symptoms.
- In-lab Polysomnography (PSG): an overnight, supervised sleep study for people with more complex medical histories.
The key measurement from these tests is the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI), which counts breathing interruptions per hour of sleep.

## AHI severity scale
- Below 5: Normal
- 5–14: Mild sleep apnea
- 15–29: Moderate sleep apnea
- 30 and above: Severe sleep apnea

## Sleep apnea treatment options
Treatment approaches described on the ResMed site include:
- CPAP (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure) therapy — the most widely used and best-understood treatment for OSA.
- Other airway pressure devices — APAP (auto-adjusting pressure) and Bilevel PAP (different pressure for inhale vs. exhale) for people who struggle with standard CPAP.
- Lifestyle changes — weight loss, reducing alcohol, quitting smoking.
- Oral appliance therapy — a mandibular repositioning device (MRD) that holds the jaw forward to keep the airway open; often used as an alternative when CPAP isn't tolerated.
- Medication review — some medications (antihistamines, opiates, benzodiazepines) can worsen symptoms.
- Nasal decongestants — for people whose sleep apnea is aggravated by congestion or allergies.
- Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation / Upper Airway Stimulation (HGNS/UAS) — for moderate-to-severe cases that haven't responded to CPAP or oral appliances.
- Surgery — e.g., Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP), nasal/septum surgery, tonsil/adenoid removal, or somnoplasty; generally not a first-line option.

## Living with sleep apnea — practical tips
ResMed recommends: staying in regular contact with your doctor, using CPAP consistently every night, maintaining a healthy weight, reducing alcohol intake (especially before bed), quitting smoking, and treating allergies that cause nasal congestion.

## CPAP devices — how they work
A CPAP device delivers a steady stream of pressurized air through a mask worn over the nose and/or mouth during sleep. This constant airflow keeps the airway open, preventing the throat tissue collapses that cause pauses in breathing. The pressure is calibrated to the individual's needs. A CPAP setup has three main parts: the device itself, tubing/hose, and a mask (nasal masks, nasal pillow masks, or full-face masks, depending on comfort and breathing style). Many modern CPAP devices include heated humidification to reduce dryness and irritation. CPAP is usually prescribed after a sleep study confirms a sleep apnea diagnosis, and consistent nightly use is important for it to work well.

## Insomnia
Insomnia is difficulty falling or staying asleep on a regular basis, which can affect daytime functioning and overall health. ResMed's site covers what insomnia is, what can cause or worsen it, and treatment/prevention approaches, alongside its sleep assessment tools.

## Snoring
Snoring happens when relaxed throat/neck muscles narrow the airway, causing tissue to vibrate as air passes through. ResMed's site explains why people snore, the impact snoring can have (on the snorer and people around them), and practical ways to reduce or stop snoring.

## Respiratory care and COPD
Beyond sleep apnea, ResMed supports people with lower-airway (lung) conditions, including Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), Obesity Hypoventilation Syndrome (OHS), restrictive lung disease/chest wall disorders, and neuromuscular disease — plus respiratory support options for children. Treatment options include cloud-connected ventilators and non-invasive ventilation (NIV) solutions for COPD.
